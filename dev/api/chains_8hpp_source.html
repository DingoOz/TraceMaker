3// How deep a rip-up chain each open connection needs (doc 20 §2.9, `tracemaker chains`): a measurement, not a router.
4//
5// The designer-oracle test (bench/records/oracle-2026-11) showed that on many boards every open connection can be drawn once the
6// other routes are arranged differently. This measures how much has to be rearranged, one open connection at a time, with the rest of
7// the routed board fixed:
8// depth 0 the connection routes on the board as it is (left by the order or the budget);
9// joint it and its blocking nets, lifted near its path, all route when the router is given them together (tried first);
10// depth 1 it routes once the blocking nets are lifted near its path, and every lifted net routes again around it;
11// depth d some lifted net needs its own blockers lifted, and so on (an ejection chain; Shin and Sangiovanni-Vincentelli's "weak
12// modification", IEEE TCAD 6(6), 1987, is the ancestor).
13// The blockers are the nets the router's last rejected route would have ripped (RouteResult::Unrouted::blockers), or failing that the
14// nets whose copper the connection's route on the bare board touches; the set is then shrunk greedily (a net is put back if the
15// connection still routes), so it is minimal, not minimum. A connection the chain has put in is held: deeper levels route around it
16// (else two nets swap places for ever). There is no backtracking: a chain that fails here might close in another
17// order. Every routing is the product router's, so a chain that closes is a legal board with one more connection routed.
