\begin{abstract}
\noindent TraceMaker reads KiCad 4--10 boards directly, routes them with an exact-geometry lattice router
that learns from its own failures, and writes the tracks and vias back into the \texttt{.kicad\_pcb} file
without disturbing anything else. It also places components with an analytic placer checked by the router.
Every result is judged by KiCad's own design-rule checker (\texttt{kicad-cli pcb drc}). On Freerouting's PCBench
benchmark set TraceMaker routes 100\% of the sampled tier-A boards cleanly, 70.0\% of tier B, 63.3\% of tier C and
54.5\% of tier D. Freerouting 2.5.0-RC12's published results on the same boards are 100\%, 50.0\%, 46.7\% and
36.4\%. No board in the final runs has a DRC error introduced by the router. On 60 boards never used during
development, with Freerouting 2.5.0-RC12 and 1.9.0 run on the same machine and judged by the same KiCad DRC,
TraceMaker's results are clean on 73--78\% of boards against 3\% and 32\%, with 5\% shorter tracks and 28\% fewer
bends than Freerouting 2.5, but more vias. This edition keeps all of that and adds Section~\ref{sec:m14}, on the
work of 7--12 October: routing of ball-grid-array (BGA) breakout boards and SoC fan-outs from tscircuit's public
benchmarks. On those, TraceMaker now solves 191 of 200 boards of \texttt{srj19} (tscircuit's router: 184) and 160 of 200
of \texttt{srj20} (tscircuit: 157; 156 within 330\,s per board) and 60 of 72 \texttt{fanout31} cases (tscircuit:
48). The tier results of Section~\ref{sec:results} were measured on release 0.8.0 and were not repeated on the new
code; the sets that bound the router's behaviour on ordinary boards (30 boards of the \texttt{quick} tier and 11 of
\texttt{mid}) give byte-identical output before and after the changes. The companion document
\texttt{report/roadmap\_testing.pdf} lists every milestone, test and benchmark run up to release 0.8.0.
\end{abstract}

\section{What TraceMaker does}

A printed circuit board (PCB) starts as a set of footprints with copper pads and a netlist that says which pads
must be connected. The router must draw copper tracks (and vias between layers) that make every connection while
respecting the board's design rules: minimum clearances between different nets, track widths, hole sizes, the
board edge, solder-mask openings and more. A route is only useful if the board then passes the design-rule check
(DRC) of the EDA tool the designer uses. TraceMaker therefore treats KiCad's DRC as the judge.

Figure~\ref{fig:beforeafter} shows the task on a real board from the benchmark set: a three-channel motor driver
with fine-pitch QFN packages. The left panel shows the input, with the required connections drawn as straight
airwires. The right panel shows TraceMaker's result, which KiCad's DRC reports as complete with no violations.

\paragraph{Lossless KiCad I/O.} The reader parses the board into an s-expression tree that remembers the byte span
of every node. Edits (adding a track, moving a footprint) replace or append spans; everything else is written back
byte for byte. All 3,538 fixture boards in the test set round-trip identically. The reader handles KiCad 4 to 10
formats, including legacy arcs, numbered and named nets, custom layer names and project rule files.

\paragraph{Exact geometry.} All coordinates are 64-bit integer nanometres, as in KiCad. Shapes are ``rounded
cores'': a point, segment or polygon inflated by a radius, which represents round pads, tracks, vias and rounded
rectangles exactly. Distance predicates use 128-bit integer arithmetic, so a clearance test never depends on
floating-point rounding.

\paragraph{A KiCad-equivalent rule engine.} Clearance is not a single number in KiCad. It is the maximum of the
board minimum, the net-class clearances of both items, local pad and footprint overrides, diff-pair gaps and
custom rules, with special cases for holes, the board edge and solder-mask openings. Each rule TraceMaker
implements was checked against \texttt{kicad-cli} on real boards (Section~\ref{sec:testing}). Examples found this
way include: plated through-holes are on every copper layer whatever the file says; non-plated holes carry the
board-edge clearance; a solder-mask opening exposes copper of its pad's net on both sides; mask graphics grow by
the board's mask expansion; copper text is an obstacle.

\section{How the router works}

\subsection{Search on an octilinear lattice}

The board is covered by a lattice of points per copper layer. The pitch is a sixth of the smallest
\emph{track width + clearance} on the board (25--100\,\textmu m), so lattice tracks fit between fine-pitch pads.
From each point a track may move in eight directions (0\textdegree, 45\textdegree, 90\textdegree, \ldots) or
change layer through a via. A connection is found with A* search, which expands lattice points in order of
\begin{equation}
  f(n) = g(n) + h(n),
\end{equation}
where $g(n)$ is the cost so far and $h(n)$ a lower bound on the remaining cost. The step cost is
\begin{equation}
  c(u\to v) = \ell(u,v) \;+\; [\text{via}]\,c_\text{via} \;+\; [\text{bend}]\,c_\text{bend}
  \;+\; h_\text{hist}(v) \;+\; [\text{crosses other copper}]\,c_\text{soft},
\end{equation}
with track length $\ell$, a via cost $c_\text{via}$ (1\,mm of track by default), an optional bend penalty,
the congestion history $h_\text{hist}$ of Section~\ref{sec:learning} and, during negotiation only, a cost for
crossing other connections' copper. The heuristic $h$ is the octile distance to the target or, on large search
windows, an exact cost-to-go field computed on the GPU (Section~\ref{sec:gpu}).

\paragraph{Legality comes from the rule engine.} Whether a lattice point is usable for a track of half-width $w/2$
on a given net is decided by the same rule engine as the DRC: the disk of radius $w/2$ around the point must keep
the required clearance from every other net's copper, from holes, the board edge, keepouts, solder-mask
openings and copper text. Results for fixed copper are cached per net class and width; routed copper sits in a
separate spatial index, and a count raster lets the search skip that index far from any routed copper.

\paragraph{Nothing is committed without an exact check.} A lattice path is converted to straight segments
(merging collinear steps) and every segment and via is verified exactly against the rule engine before it is
written. If the check fails, the offending lattice points are learned as blocked for that net and the search is
repeated. Short ``escape stubs'' off the lattice connect fine-pitch pads whose centres do not sit on lattice
points.

\subsection{Learning from failure}
\label{sec:learning}

The requirement was to ``try the optimal first, store the failure information and use it to improve the next
attempt''. TraceMaker does this at four levels.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Within a search.} A failed search reports \emph{why}: the source or target pad is boxed in (the
  open list ran out without reaching the window edge), the window was too small, the search budget ran out, or
  the exact check rejected the path. A boxed-in target is detected with a short reverse search from the target.
  The reason picks the next step instead of blindly retrying.
  \item \textbf{Escalation ladder per connection.} Strict routing first (no crossing of other copper); then forced
  off-lattice escapes for pads with no lattice exit; then a neck-down to the board's minimum track width; then
  negotiation.
  \item \textbf{Negotiated rip-up (PathFinder).} In negotiation a path may cross other connections' copper at a cost.
  The crossed connections are ripped up and queued again, and every contested lattice point accumulates a history
  cost
  \begin{equation}
    h_\text{hist}(v) \leftarrow h_\text{hist}(v) + 1 \quad\text{whenever } v \text{ is contested},
  \end{equation}
  so that later searches avoid congested places. This is the PathFinder idea of McMurchie and Ebeling (1995).
  ``Nogoods'' record attempts that failed in exactly the same surroundings, so they are not repeated, and the best
  legal state seen so far is always kept. When negotiation stalls, the router restarts from an empty board with
  the hardest connections first, keeping the history; once the planned restarts are used, further restarts clear
  the nogoods, halve the history and shuffle ties while budget remains.
  \item \textbf{Across runs.} A SQLite knowledge base stores, per class of board, how well each router variant did
  and which connections failed on each board. A Thompson-sampling bandit chooses variants: each variant $k$ has a
  Beta$(\alpha_k,\beta_k)$ posterior over its success rate, a sample $\theta_k$ is drawn for each, and the variants
  with the largest samples run. Connections that failed before are routed first next time.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Clean-up after routing}
\label{sec:cleanup}

Search on a lattice finds legal paths, but not tidy ones: equal-cost staircases of short 45\textdegree\ steps, and vias
placed where a slightly longer single-layer route existed. Once routing is done, TraceMaker therefore cleans up,
much as Freerouting's optimizer does:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Via-saving re-routes.} Connections that use vias are taken out one at a time, most vias first, and
  routed again with vias made ten times dearer and other connections' copper as a hard obstacle. The new route is kept
  only if it is cheaper by track length plus 2\,mm per via; otherwise the old copper is restored exactly. Rounds
  repeat while connections improve and search budget remains.
  \item \textbf{Path smoothing (``pull tight'').} Within each connection, runs of same-layer, same-width segments are
  replaced by the fewest octilinear segments that the exact rule check accepts, keeping pads and vias where they
  are. This step is pure geometry, cheap and deterministic, so it always runs.
\end{enumerate}
Every new segment passes the same exact check as during routing, so clean-up cannot introduce a DRC error.

\subsection{Parallel portfolio and determinism}

Different strategies suit different boards: short connections first or long first, cheap or expensive vias,
exact bend tracking or fast bends, on large boards a lattice of double pitch and on small boards one of half
pitch. TraceMaker runs a portfolio of
eight such variants in parallel threads and keeps the best result (most connections, then fewest vias, then
shortest copper). When one variant has routed everything after time $T$, the others may continue until $2T + 5$\,s, so
a cleaner complete result can still win.

Runs can be made fully deterministic with a work budget (\texttt{--work}, counted in A* expansions) instead of a
time limit: random choices use a counter-based Philox generator keyed by (seed, stage, item), hash containers are
never iterated in an output-affecting order, and costs are integers. Repeated runs then produce byte-identical
boards at any thread count, which the regression tests rely on.

\subsection{GPU cost-to-go fields}
\label{sec:gpu}

On large search windows A* spends most of its time exploring around obstacles that a straight-line estimate does
not see. Before such a search, a CUDA kernel computes the exact obstacle-aware cost to the target from every
lattice point of the window (iterated GAMER-style sweeps over the grid). This field is a lower bound that is much
tighter than the octile distance, so A* expands far fewer points. The field never prunes: where it claims
``unreachable'', the router falls back to the octile bound, so the search stays complete. A CPU implementation
produces bit-identical fields, and the tests compare them. Because the GPUs are shared with other jobs, a variant
stops building fields if they take more than 30\% of its wall-clock time.

\subsection{Component placement}

Placement (\texttt{tracemaker-place}) works in stages: a quadratic wirelength minimisation with the bound-to-bound
net model, solved by conjugate gradients; SimPL-style spreading to remove overlap; rotation choice; legalisation to
the nearest legal position (courtyards, board outline, copper and solder-mask clearances); and parallel simulated
annealing on half-perimeter wirelength
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{HPWL} = \sum_{\text{nets } e} \Big(\max_{p\in e} x_p - \min_{p\in e} x_p + \max_{p\in e} y_p - \min_{p\in e} y_p\Big)
\end{equation}
plus a penalty per airwire crossing. A linear-programming lower bound on HPWL, solved as a min-cost flow, is reported
alongside so the quality of each placement can be judged.

Shorter wiring does not automatically mean a board routes better: denser packing can block escape routes. The
placer therefore puts the router in the loop. With \texttt{--route-check}, the input placement and the new one are
routed with the same deterministic budget, and the new placement is kept only if it leaves no more connections
unrouted. \texttt{--mode auto} does this for both the refine and the full mode and keeps the best of the three.

\subsection{Live view and KiCad integration}

\texttt{tracemaker route --view} streams the routing as it happens to a WebGL2 viewer in the browser: tracks
appearing and being ripped up, the A* frontier, failures and the remaining airwires. A KiCad~10 action plugin
routes the open board through KiCad's IPC API and adds the result in one undoable commit; it can also re-route
existing unlocked copper, move footprints first and refill zones.

\section{How it was tested}
\label{sec:testing}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Unit and integration tests} (262 in the current code, run with \texttt{ctest}; release 0.8.0 had 132): exact unit conversion, Philox known-answer vectors, GPU-equals-CPU for the random generator and
  cost-to-go fields, s-expression parsing and byte-exact round trips, the board reader against KiCad's own model,
  rule reading, geometry predicates and the indexed paths against their linear references, placement legality and
  lower bounds, the component-rule catalogue and impedance formulas, differential-pair geometry, and the viewer
  server and replay log, and since 0.8.0 the Simple Route JSON importer, bus routing and the after-route passes.
  The suite passes in the release build (262 of 262 on 11 October, after merging main). The address-sanitizer build compiled and passed
  all tests it finished; \texttt{bus\_route} and \texttt{thread\_determinism} are too slow under it and were not run to
  completion for this edition.
  \item \textbf{DRC parity.} TraceMaker's own DRC is compared with \texttt{kicad-cli} on the KiCad demo boards: 14
  of 17 match exactly on the 15 routing-relevant violation types, and 264 of 264 boards with injected defects match
  violation by violation; both are regression tests. The router uses the same rule engine, so parity problems show
  up as router errors and were fixed at the source.
  \item \textbf{Benchmark harness.} \texttt{bench/run.py} samples boards from Freerouting's PCBench set by
  Freerouting's own difficulty tiers, routes each with a snapshot of the binary (120\,s, eight variants), and runs
  \texttt{kicad-cli pcb drc} on the result. A board is \emph{clean} when it is fully connected and KiCad reports no
  violation involving a track, via or arc that was not already present in the unrouted board. The result is
  compared with Freerouting's published per-board results for the same files.
  \item \textbf{Determinism.} With a work budget the output is byte-identical at any thread count and with or
  without a GPU; a test routes the same board at one, three and eight threads and compares the files.
  \item \textbf{A running KiCad.} The plugin is driven against \texttt{pcbnew} on a virtual display: copper appears
  in the open board, one undo removes it, and KiCad's DRC of the saved board shows no new error.
\end{itemize}

\section{What was added after the first version}
\label{sec:since}

The first version of this report described the state on 2--3 October. This section summarises what was built and
measured between then and release 0.8.0; \texttt{report/roadmap\_testing.pdf} has the numbers behind each item.

\paragraph{Routing features.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Escape planning} for dense packages: reserved exit corridors for perimeter pins and dog-bone sites
  for inner balls, used by two of the eight portfolio variants, and \texttt{tracemaker escape}, which reports pins
  that cannot leave their package under the board's rules. A flow-based planner for deep arrays was built and
  measured below the simple corridors; it is opt-in.
  \item \textbf{Differential pairs} routed as one object: a coupled search with coupled vias, breakout legs,
  re-coupling after rip-up, skew tuning, and twists (a layer change with the sides swapped) for pairs whose pin
  order is mirrored between the ends. On 18 boards with 59 pairs, 36 are coupled over at least half their length;
  the median coupled share is 63\,\%.
  \item \textbf{Length tuning} to KiCad \texttt{length} rules by meanders; blind, buried and micro vias where the
  board's rules allow them.
  \item \textbf{Connection planning by copper distance:} each net's spanning tree weighs pads by the gap between
  their copper, not their centres.
  \item \textbf{A reachability pre-check:} an exact flood fill proves a search hopeless before the A* runs.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Component-aware rules.} TraceMaker recognises common parts (USB, Ethernet, crystals, regulators and
others) from footprints and nets, and applies a catalogue of 193 layout rules drawn from application notes:
proximity pulls in placement (on by default), generated keep-outs, and with \texttt{--component-rules on} net
classes whose widths come from impedance targets and the board's stackup or from current.

\paragraph{Placement.} Parts can be moved to the other side of the board (opt-in), with mirroring that matches
KiCad's exactly. For boards with no placement at all, \texttt{--scratch} places every movable part from a pile.
This was measured on a held-out set built for the purpose: 40 PCBench boards never used before, with their
movable parts piled at the board centre.

\paragraph{Correctness and speed.} Two faults were found by those held-out boards and fixed: copper had been routed

a byte-identical report.

\paragraph{KiCad integration.} The plugin was run against a live \texttt{pcbnew} on a virtual display, and its
package was installed through KiCad's Plugin and Content Manager (Section~\ref{sec:testing}).

\paragraph{What did not help.} Global routing was built twice (corridors, then a congestion map with net-shared
edges and via capacity) and measured on 18 hard boards at a fixed budget: corridors cost completion (13,305

is more than 40\,\% full: the boards are not short of space, and what fails is the fan-out at a fine-pitch package
under rules coarser than the original design's.

\section{BGA breakout and SoC fan-out (7--12 October)}
\label{sec:m14}

\paragraph{The task.} tscircuit, an open-source electronics toolkit, publishes the boards its autorouter is tested
against. Two sets are ball-grid-array breakouts on two copper layers: \texttt{srj19} (200 boards, MIT licence) and
\texttt{srj20} (200 boards with some balls missing, no licence, used locally only). A third, \texttt{fanout31}, has 72
fan-out cases of six SoC packages (AM62L, RK3308, K230, i.MX6ULL, T113-S3, AM3352) on 8--10 layers with plane
terminations, buses and skew goals. The goal was to route at least as many of them as tscircuit's router does,
without adding a design-rule error to any board. Their published figures (dashboard run \texttt{38105514608-1}) are
92.0\,\% of \texttt{srj19}, 78.5\,\% of \texttt{srj20} and 48 of 72 \texttt{fanout31} cases.

\paragraph{How it was judged.} Each problem is imported as a self-contained KiCad board (\texttt{tracemaker
srj-import}), routed by the ordinary pipeline, and judged by \texttt{kicad-cli pcb drc} at the distances of tscircuit's
own pass criterion (0.1\,mm copper clearance, 0.1\,mm between via holes). The routed copper can be exported back to the
Simple Route JSON format (\texttt{srj-export}), where tscircuit's checker gives a second score. A board counts as
solved only when it is fully connected with no router-introduced violation. All work budgets are counted in search
steps, so a run is reproducible and independent of the machine.

\subsection{Research before building}

The plan (\texttt{docs/16-bga-breakout-plan.md}) and the evidence behind it (\texttt{research/bga-routing-2026-10.md})
rest on the literature on escape routing and on tscircuit's own public code and benchmark data. Three points shaped
the work. First, the gap between the two routers is a cliff: boards with up to about 40 connections are solved by both,
and the difference sits in boards of 50--79 connections. Second, escape feasibility for each ball separately does not
imply that all balls can leave together, which is why exact methods for ordered escape routing (SAT, flow models with
diagonal capacity) exist. Third, tscircuit's router uses many vias outside the ball array. The research note also
catalogues approaches reported not to work (12 from the literature, 8 from tscircuit's history, 8 from TraceMaker's
earlier milestones), so they were not repeated.

\subsection{What worked}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Higher rip and pass limits.} Allowing each connection to be ripped up 40 times and the negotiation 40
  passes (were 8 and 12) raised the clean share of the 24 \texttt{srj19} boards that one variant first missed from 33\,\% to 54\,\%
  at equal work. These are now the defaults; \texttt{quick} and \texttt{mid} are byte-identical.
  \item \textbf{Fast bends} (cheaper search around bends): at equal wall time 81.7\,\% to 85.9\,\% of connections routed
  on the same boards.
  \item \textbf{Preferred layer directions and a package penalty.} Orthogonal steps against a layer's preferred
  direction (even layers horizontal, odd layers vertical) cost extra, and tracks on the pad layer inside a ball
  array cost more. This was the first structural gain on \texttt{srj20} (Figure~\ref{fig:m14dir}). On ordinary
  two-layer boards the same settings cost one to three points of completion (\texttt{quick}: 89.2 to 86.8\,\%), so
  they are applied automatically only to a \emph{breakout board}: two copper layers, at least 70\,\% of the
  connections ending on a ball-grid-like package (at least 30 same-layer SMD pads on at least $5\times5$ positions with 40\,\% of
  the grid filled). A first version of this test also matched the connectors of a bus-routing test board and broke
  it; requiring the grid shape fixed that.
  \item \textbf{A larger portfolio.} Beyond the designed eight variants, \texttt{--variants N} adds seeded variants
  with different orders and via and crossing costs. Sixteen variants close 7 of 19 \texttt{srj19} boards and 9 of 76
  \texttt{srj20} boards that one variant misses.
  \item \textbf{A corrected fan-out model.} The first import of the K230 cases routed none of 12. The importer had
  overridden the layer of each breakout point with the layer named for its bus, but several buses' breakout points share
  coordinates and differ only by layer, so the override stacked two or three terminals on one layer and made them
  unreachable. Dropping the override (the importer's other features stayed) took K230 to 12 of 12. Also needed:
  plane terminations as zones, pads of nets with no connection point as keep-outs, and a board-outline margin.
\end{itemize}

The second round ended 24 boards short on \texttt{srj20}. Rendering a hard board's progress showed why: the routed count of the negotiation phase
wobbles by $\pm5$ around one level for the whole work budget (30 to 41 of 53 connections on \texttt{sample069}, ending at 30; only the best snapshot is
returned). A connection that cannot be routed rips the one to ten connections in its way, those cannot all be routed again, and the next one rips them
back: the oscillation that negotiated-congestion routers are known for, and what multi-agent path finding calls a plan with collisions.
MAPF-LNS2 (Li et al., AAAI 2022) repairs such plans by re-planning chosen agents and keeping the change only if the collisions do not increase.
TraceMaker's version:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Transactions.} A negotiated route that rips victims is kept only if the victims can be routed again \emph{strictly} (without ripping
  anything) and the net change in routed connections is not negative; otherwise a snapshot of every connection's copper is restored and the history
  of the failed try stays, so the next try takes another path. A loss of $k$ connections is still accepted with probability $e^{-k/T}$ ($T=1.5$,
  seeded draws, so runs stay deterministic). The same board goes from 41 to 50 of 53 connections at equal work.
  \item \textbf{A budget that charges what costs time.} The work budget counted a reachability flood cell like an A* expansion, but a flood costs a
  small fraction of the time. With floods before every strict search and counted at a quarter, the same nominal budget buys about twice the search.
  \item \textbf{Fields kept.} The cost-to-go field was 47\,\% of the run time and repeats for each connection once the fixed-copper cache is complete;
  keeping it (the same copper, checked by byte comparison) saves 38\,\% of the wall time.
  \item \textbf{A portfolio made for breakouts, in rounds.} The designed eight variants (exact bends, longest first) are wrong for these boards. The
  breakout portfolio draws order, via cost, crossing cost, direction strength and temperature from fixed sets; after each of four rounds every variant
  continues from the best state of all.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What did not work}

Every item below was measured at equal work and kept as a hidden option with its result recorded
(\texttt{docs/16}, sections 10--11; decision D95), so nobody repeats it.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{tscircuit's own routes as path guides} (soft or hard, radii of 1.5--8 pitches) on 12 boards: 440--499 of
  570 connections against 499 without. A connection guided along another router's off-lattice geometry does not
  complete the board. \textbf{Layer assignment} by crossing count (two-layer max-cut) or chord angle: clean boards fell from 18\,\% to 11--14\,\%.
  \item \textbf{Different orders} (longest first, deep package pins first): 76.7\,\% of connections routed fell to 54.9\,\% and 68.6\,\%.
  \item \textbf{Global routing} (three modes): 69.2--76.5\,\% against 76.7\,\%. \textbf{Negotiating from the first pass:} 72.7\,\%.
  \item \textbf{Limiting how many connections one search may rip up} (1, 2, 3): clean boards fell from 28\,\% to 2, 5 and 7\,\%.
  \item \textbf{A growing crossing cost} (the PathFinder schedule), \textbf{history weights} 2--100: worse or no effect.
  \item \textbf{Weighted A*} (heuristic weight 1.5, 2.5): 4\,\% clean. Path quality matters here.
  \item \textbf{Cheaper vias} alone (3 down to 0.3\,mm): clean boards 0 to 2 of 24; \textbf{finer lattices}: 20\,\textmu m was worse than 40 at equal work.
  \item \textbf{A polishing phase} that reserves part of the budget to rebuild the neighbourhoods of the last open
  connections: closed 0--1 of 3--6 and lost more in the shortened main phase. \textbf{24 seeded variants on one hard board} all stopped at 33--40 of 52 connections.
  \item \textbf{More work} helps only a little: 200 million units on the 33 remaining \texttt{srj20} gap boards closed 6.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Where \texttt{srj20} and \texttt{srj19} stand}

Of the 40 \texttt{srj20} boards not completed, 28 have 70 or more connections and are 8--40 connections short (tscircuit solves two of them); the
other 12 have 1--6 open. On \texttt{srj19}, 9 boards remain. Neither set is at 100\,\%: some of the remaining boards may have no solution under the
rules, and nobody has published a feasibility bound.
The earlier diagnosis (\texttt{sample069}, 188 vias for tscircuit, 173 of them outside the ball array) still stands as the difference in style.

\subsection{Other changes since release 0.8.0}

Besides the BGA work, the current code contains changes that were made after the 6 October edition:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Simple Route JSON} support at the edge of the engine (\texttt{srj-import}, \texttt{srj-export}) and
  benchmark scripts for the tscircuit sets (decision D90).
  \item \textbf{Bus routing} (opt-in): the connections of a bus run in pin order, each searched inside a guide around its
  neighbour's lane, with skew tuning and a \texttt{tracemaker buses} measurement command (D91). Marked experimental.
  \item \textbf{After-route passes} (off by default): widening power tracks, stitching vias for plane return paths against an
  in-engine zone refill, and a track-style metric (D92). Zones are refilled in the engine with exact polygon operations
  that follow KiCad 10, so plane boards are judged without calling KiCad for the fills (D85).
  \item \textbf{Instrumentation:} per-phase work counters in the route report (D88).
\end{itemize}
Release 0.9.0 on the main branch also merges outside contributions (plane-aware routing of all-SMD boards, a macOS
build, layer limits for tracks, and rule-reading fixes); its tier results are recorded in the development log as
100, 72.5, 60.0 and 54.5\,\% clean on tiers A to D. The branch described in this section was cut before that release and is not
merged to main or published; the two have not been combined or re-measured together.

\section{Limitations and next steps}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The last few connections at fine-pitch packages.} Most boards that are not clean miss a handful of
  connections where two or three nets keep displacing each other at one package, or where the vias a crossing needs
  do not fit under KiCad's default rules. Neither global routing nor re-ordering helped; a lattice of half the
  pitch helps a little (one more clean board in tier B) at four times the work.
  \item \textbf{Not 100\,\% on the tscircuit sets:} 9 \texttt{srj19} and 40 \texttt{srj20} boards stay open, 28 of them with 70 or more
  connections (Section~\ref{sec:m14}). \texttt{fanout31} cases 55--57 (T113-S3) and 61--69 (AM3352) are unsolved.
  \item \textbf{Large boards are time-limited.} On the biggest boards the router finishes only one or two negotiation
  passes in 120\,s.
  \item \textbf{From-scratch placement is at 85\,\% clean, not the 90\,\% aimed for,} and a part that cannot be placed
  legally is set down beside the board with a message.
  \item \textbf{Dense packages:} 41\,\% of the 17 dense-package boards are clean (Freerouting 2.5: 24\,\%).
  \item \textbf{Edge-connector fingers under solder-mask openings} are left unrouted rather than routed with
  mask bridges. Freerouting's published ``clean'' counts only clearance violations and unrouted connections, so on
  these boards TraceMaker is judged more strictly.
  \item \textbf{More vias than Freerouting.} Even after clean-up TraceMaker uses about 1.4 times Freerouting 2.5's vias
  and 2.2 times Freerouting 1.9's (median, held-out boards), and it narrows some tracks below the design width.
  \item \textbf{The tier statistics use Freerouting's published numbers,} which allowed up to 30 minutes per board and
  are judged by Freerouting's own DRC. The held-out quality benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:quality}) runs both
  Freerouting versions locally and judges them with KiCad, but covers 60 boards and was not repeated for this
  edition.
  \item \textbf{One width per net.} Net classes from impedance rules use the front-layer width on every layer.
  \item \textbf{Copper text} is modelled by generous estimated boxes rather than exact glyph outlines.
  \item \textbf{Not measured:} the placer against the SA-PCB annealer (that baseline did not build on the test
  machine), and the viewer's frame rate.
  \item \textbf{Not repeated on the current code:} the tier benchmark, the held-out quality study and the placement
  study (Sections~\ref{sec:results}, \ref{sec:quality}, \ref{sec:since}); their numbers are those of release 0.8.0.
  \item \textbf{Not built:} a planner that fixes layers and via sites before routing, a gridless routing arm, learned net ordering, a GPU global router, per-layer track
  widths, and a schematic reader of TraceMaker's own (netlists come from \texttt{kicad-cli}).
\end{itemize}
